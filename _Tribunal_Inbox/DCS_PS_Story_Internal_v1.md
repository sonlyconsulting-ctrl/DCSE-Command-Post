## A NOTE BEFORE THE FIRST CHAPTER

This document is written in two voices because the story lives in two registers.

It opens in the third person -- the way a journalist opens a 60 Minutes segment, establishing context before the subject speaks. It then moves into the first person, because what happened next cannot be told from a distance.

Some names are institutional. Some conversations happened between a man and an AI model at two in the morning. Some arguments were first drafted in a chat window and later filed in a federal court. All of it is true. All of it is documented. All of it is part of the record.

This is not a story about victimhood. It is a story about what happens when someone who has spent thirty years building systems decides to build one more -- this time, to prove what was done to him.

## CHAPTER 1: THE ASSIGNMENT

### August 24, 2020

[PULL QUOTE: "He had been here before -- not this office, not this state, but this moment. The moment before the work begins."]

The email arrived the way most opportunities do: quietly, without fanfare, tucked between the ordinary traffic of a professional inbox. Contract Staffing Specialists had a placement. The Nebraska Department of Health and Human Services needed an Aion developer. Senior level. The NFOCUS system -- Nebraska's human services information platform -- was undergoing modernization. They needed someone who knew the architecture of legacy systems and could write the code to bring them forward.

Donald C. Seals, Jr. had been doing this for thirty years.

He accepted the assignment.

On August 24, 2020, Donald Seals began work at the Nebraska DHHS in Lincoln. He was fifty-four years old. He had worked for state agencies before, for federal contractors, for enterprise organizations building and rebuilding the data infrastructure that makes government function. He understood legacy codebases the way a structural engineer understands an old building -- not just what it was designed to do, but why it was built the way it was, and what it would take to make it last another generation.

He was the only African American on the team.

That fact registered. It always does, for people who carry it. Not as a threat in the first moment, but as information. The kind of information that a person with thirty years of professional experience learns to hold without letting it alter the work.

The work was what mattered. And the work was substantial.

The NFOCUS system is not a peripheral application. It is the operational backbone of Nebraska's child welfare and human services infrastructure -- the system through which caseworkers manage cases, track children, document placements, and coordinate the full range of state human services. Modernizing it was not a cosmetic project. It was essential work, and the team assigned to it knew it.

Donald Seals knew it too. He came in, got his credentials, received his state-issued laptop, was given his DHHS email address, was badged into the building, and was assigned to the Expert Systems group under the direct supervision of Kim Converse.

He reported to Converse for daily task assignments. He submitted his hours through the Clarity timekeeping system, where Converse approved them. Julie Christensen, the IT Applications Developer Lead in the DHHS Chief Information Officer's office, served as his manager. Lisa Schafers operated at the executive level above them both.

From the outside, and from the inside, this looked like employment.

The State of Nebraska's position, formally and legally, was that it was not.

### The Architecture of the Arrangement

[PULL QUOTE: "The structure was designed to create distance. But distance and control do not occupy the same space."]

To understand what happened next, you need to understand the structure that was placed between Donald Seals and the institution that directed his work.

The State of Nebraska does not hire IT contractors directly. It operates through a vendor intermediary -- in this case, Covendis Technologies, which managed the staffing arrangement under a state contract. Covendis placed the actual employment relationship with Contract Staffing Specialists, Inc., a firm based in Omaha. CSS was the entity that issued Donald Seals' paychecks. CSS was the entity that appeared on his W-2. CSS was the entity that, on paper, employed him.

DHHS interviewed him. DHHS selected him. DHHS gave him his computer. DHHS gave him his email address. DHHS gave him his badge. DHHS assigned his work. DHHS approved his hours. DHHS decided what he would build and when he would build it. DHHS decided he would no longer be there.

This arrangement has a name in employment law. It is called a joint employer relationship, or more precisely, it is analyzed under what the Supreme Court established in Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden in 1992 -- a multi-factor common law agency test that looks past the formal payroll relationship to ask who actually controlled the work.

None of this was visible to Donald Seals on August 24, 2020. He was not thinking about Title VII. He was thinking about NFOCUS. He was thinking about the iServe integration. He was thinking about Aion, the business rules language he had been hired to work in, and about the architecture of a system that had been built across decades and needed careful hands to move it forward.

He began building.

## CHAPTER 2: INSIDE THE MACHINE

### The Work, The Team, The Weeks

[PULL QUOTE: "Thirty years does not mean you have seen everything. It means you know what you are seeing when you see it."]

The weeks that followed settled into a rhythm that anyone who has worked in government IT will recognize. The systems were complex, the timelines were sometimes unrealistic, and the work required a certain patience -- the patience of someone who understands that a legacy codebase is not a problem to be solved but a structure to be understood.

Donald Seals understood it.

He was assigned to NFOCUS module development. He worked on the iServe integration. His work was reviewed and accepted by DHHS standards and workflows. His outputs remained in DHHS-controlled repositories. His deliverables were subject to DHHS approval processes. When his supervisor needed something, she asked him. When the system needed something, he built it.

He also noticed things.

He noticed that his assignments shifted. Early on, his work was substantive -- direct engagement with the systems he had been hired to develop. As the weeks progressed, the assignments became less consistent. Work he had been given was reassigned to other members of the team. When he volunteered for assignments, his offers were declined. There were weeks, in late November and early December 2020, when a week or more would pass without a meaningful assignment.

He also noticed the overtime meeting.

In late November or early December 2020, Kim Converse announced in a team meeting that overtime had been authorized for the group. Donald Seals was the only member of the team who did not receive overtime approval. His Caucasian colleagues did.

He was the only African American on the team.

He said nothing in the meeting. He continued working. He documented what he observed.

### The Data That Did Not Lie

[PULL QUOTE: "The Clarity system recorded everything. DHHS hoped it would record nothing relevant. It recorded everything relevant."]

Among the materials produced in discovery in this litigation -- years later, after federal complaints and court filings and disputes over privilege logs and motions for relief -- was a set of native exports from the DHHS Clarity timekeeping system.

Clarity is the platform DHHS used to track contractor hours. Every time a contractor submitted hours, every time a supervisor approved them, the transaction was recorded. The metadata was captured. The approval chain was documented.

The discovery production included Clarity exports for Donald Seals and for a group of other contractors who worked in the same general period, under the same supervisory structure. Among them was a contractor named Mark Traub -- white, working on overlapping NFOCUS and iServe projects, sharing the same supervisors, working in the same environment.

The numbers told a story that no witness could contradict because no witness had written them. Traub received overtime approval in fourteen of thirty-seven weeks tracked. That is 37.8 percent. Donald Seals received overtime approval in zero of his tracked weeks. Zero percent.

Same system. Same supervisors approving the hours. Different contractors. Different outcomes. Race was the variable that was not supposed to be visible in the data. The data made it visible anyway.

There was also Trent Ballentine.

Ballentine began his assignment at DHHS on the same date as Donald Seals -- August 24, 2020. He was white. He worked in the same programming language -- a specific and unusual one, Aion, that was not a common skill in the market. He reported to Kim Converse. He was managed by Julie Christensen. He operated under the executive oversight of Lisa Schafers.

He was retained when Donald Seals was removed.

This is what the Eighth Circuit calls a comparator. An individual who was similarly situated in all material respects -- same supervisor, same manager, same start date, same technical environment, same team -- who received different treatment. The difference in treatment correlates to race. That correlation, in a federal courtroom, is the beginning of a discrimination case.

## CHAPTER 3: THE SILENCE BEFORE THE CUT

### What Was Not Written Down

[PULL QUOTE: "An employer who watched eight months of work and wrote nothing -- nothing -- is an employer who made a decision for reasons that could not be written down."]

In employment discrimination law, there is a concept that practitioners sometimes call the paper trail problem. The paper trail problem has two directions. The first is when an employer over-documents -- creates performance records that are transparently pretextual, written after the fact to justify a decision already made. The second is when an employer produces nothing.

DHHS produced nothing.

Across Donald Seals' entire eight-month assignment at the Nebraska Department of Health and Human Services -- from August 24, 2020 through the events of January and March 2021 -- DHHS produced in discovery: zero performance evaluations. Zero coaching logs. Zero performance improvement plans. Zero written warnings. Zero documented conversations about performance concerns.

One statement.

Julie Christensen, the IT Applications Developer Lead, at some point characterized Donald Seals' performance with the phrase "not working out."

That is the entirety of DHHS's contemporaneous performance record for a senior IT professional with thirty years of experience who was integrated into the state's core human services systems for eight months.

Not working out.

No date on the statement. No document that preceded it. No warning that accompanied it. No meeting where it was communicated to Seals himself. No record of what specifically was not working, or how the employer had attempted to address it, or what standard was being applied that Seals was failing to meet.

Just a phrase. Verbal. Undocumented. Applied retroactively to a decision that had already been made.

In federal employment discrimination law, this pattern has a name too. The legal scholars and practitioners who study it sometimes call it the Ghost Performance Doctrine -- the practice of asserting performance-based reasons for termination that have no documentary ghost, no paper evidence that they existed before the termination decision was made.

A decision without a paper trail is a decision someone did not want to explain.

### The Privilege Log and the Documents That Could Not Be Named

[PULL QUOTE: "They were willing to say the documents existed. They were not willing to say what was in them. That silence has legal weight."]

In November 2025, in the middle of a contested discovery battle in federal court, DHHS's attorney at the time -- Assistant Attorney General Tyrone E. Fahie -- responded to a series of questions about withheld documents.

The response revealed something.

Among the documents DHHS was withholding under claims of attorney-client privilege were meeting notes drafted by a woman named Courtney Jerke, who worked in DHHS Human Resources. The notes captured a conversation between a DHHS attorney named Grant Dugdale and the supervising employees who had managed Donald Seals -- the very supervisors who would later be questioned about his performance and the circumstances of his removal.

The conversation captured in those notes happened as DHHS was preparing its response to Donald Seals' EEOC charge.

The EEOC charge was the formal discrimination complaint Seals had filed after his assignment ended. The meeting in Courtney Jerke's notes was a meeting about how to respond to that complaint. It included the supervisors who had overseen Seals' work and whose decision-making would become the subject of the federal lawsuit that followed.

DHHS's privilege log also identified communications involving Grant Dugdale, Courtney Jerke, and Kim Converse -- a series of exchanges that occurred immediately after Seals filed his EEOC charge. The privilege log entries described these communications as privileged attorney-client communications.

Seals' legal theory is that these communications represent something else: a coordinated effort to construct a performance-based justification after a discrimination complaint had already been filed. The documents, in that theory, are not privileged legal advice. They are operational communications between HR personnel and supervisors who were not seeking legal advice but building a defense.

The documents have not been produced. The in camera review that DHHS offered -- inviting the court to look at the documents privately -- has not been completed. The trial is scheduled for September 14, 2026.

What is in those documents is unknown. What the documents' existence suggests, to the legal mind that has spent years studying this case, is something specific: when a company withholds meeting notes that were created immediately after a discrimination complaint, involving the supervisors whose conduct is being investigated, the inference the law permits is not a favorable one.

## CHAPTER 4: JANUARY 15, 2021

### The Email That Was Not Sent to Him

[PULL QUOTE: "He was the last to know what had already been decided. That is not a coincidence. That is a structure."]

On March 25, 2021, at 12:16 in the afternoon, Julie Christensen sent an email.

The email was addressed to Becky Fields at Contract Staffing Specialists. Lisa Schafers was copied.

The subject line read: Termination of Contract: 55696-0002 Senior Aion(CA) Applications Developer.

The body of the email was brief.

"As per our phone conversation, we are terminating the contract with Don Seals effective, Thursday, 3/25/2021 at 5:00 p.m."

The email continued: "He is not aware of it so please talk to him after 5:00 p.m. today."

It then instructed CSS to collect his state ID card.

Donald Seals was not on the email. He was the subject of the email. He was the person whose professional engagement was being ended at 5:00 p.m. that day. He was being instructed to return a physical credential. And he was not to be told until after 5:00.

The decision had been made without him. The communication about the decision was made without him. The instructions about what would happen to him were issued by a DHHS manager to the staffing agency that nominally employed him.

This email is Seals-TitleVII-000079. It is in the record.

It is also the document that, in a single exchange, answers the central legal question of this case.

DHHS's defense is that it was not Donald Seals' employer. Its defense is that CSS employed him, and CSS made employment decisions about him, and DHHS merely had a contract relationship with a vendor.

But Julie Christensen did not send this email to Donald Seals. She did not contact CSS to say that Seals had failed to meet CSS's employment standards. She did not request that CSS take action consistent with its own HR processes. She sent an email directing an outcome, at a specific time, with instructions for logistics.

She told CSS when to terminate his contract. She told CSS not to tell him until after 5:00. She told CSS to collect his badge.

That is not a client interacting with a vendor's employee. That is an employer ending an employment relationship. The structural wrapper of the staffing arrangement does not change what the email shows.

### What He Felt When He Found Out

[PULL QUOTE: "Some things hit differently when you have been here before. Not the specifics. The shape of it."]

It is one thing to describe what happened legally. It is another to describe what it is like to be the person to whom it happened.

Donald Seals had thirty years of professional experience. He had worked for state agencies and private contractors and federal programs. He had built systems that other people depended on. He had done this across decades, across multiple states, across technologies that had risen and fallen and risen again in a different form.

He had also been Black in professional spaces for thirty years.

He knew the shape of what happened to him. He had seen versions of it before -- the assignments that shifted, the overtime that went to everyone else, the meeting where the decision had already been made and he was the last to hear it. He had seen the shape of it. He recognized it.

What was different this time was that he was not going to let the shape of it define what came next.

He had resources that people in his position often do not have. He had thirty years of professional experience with enterprise systems, which meant he understood documentation, evidence trails, and the architecture of institutional decision-making. He had the technical fluency to look at data and see what it meant. He had the intellectual discipline to approach a legal problem the way he approached a coding problem: systematically, with structure, without letting frustration override the logic.

And he had time. And access to something that had not existed the last time something like this happened to someone like him.

He had AI.

## CHAPTER 5: THE DECISION TO FIGHT

### The EEOC Charge

[PULL QUOTE: "The first filing is not a legal document. It is a declaration. You are saying: I am here. I know what happened. I am not going away."]

On July 30, 2021, Donald C. Seals, Jr. signed and submitted a charge of discrimination to the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. He submitted it digitally. The Indiana Civil Rights Commission was listed as the co-filing agency. The basis of discrimination was race. The employer was listed as the State of Nebraska DHHS.

The particulars described what had happened: the assignment, the shift in work quality and quantity, the overtime that was announced in a meeting and not extended to him, the removal that was communicated to a staffing agency with instructions to tell him after 5:00 p.m.

He wrote it in his own words. No attorney helped him. He described what he had seen, what he had experienced, and what he believed it meant.

The EEOC process is a prerequisite for a Title VII lawsuit. Before you can sue, you have to give the agency an opportunity to investigate and potentially resolve the matter. The agency issues a right-to-sue letter when the administrative process concludes.

Donald Seals received his right-to-sue letter. He filed in federal court in the United States District Court for the District of Nebraska on October 17, 2023.

Case number 8:23CV489. Seals v. State of Nebraska Department of Health and Human Services.

He filed it himself.

### Going Pro Se

[PULL QUOTE: "Pro se does not mean alone. It means you are the one who signs the documents."]

The decision to proceed without retained counsel is not made lightly by anyone who understands what it means. Federal civil rights litigation against a state agency is among the most complex categories of civil litigation that exists. The rules are intricate. The procedural requirements are demanding. The opposing counsel -- in this case, the Office of the Nebraska Attorney General -- brings institutional resources, legal expertise, and the full weight of a state government's legal apparatus to every filing.

And yet.

There are structural realities about civil rights litigation against employers that have shaped this landscape for decades. Employment discrimination cases are expensive to litigate. Attorney fees are contingency-based or flat-fee, which means attorneys evaluate the financial viability of a case before taking it on. The damages in individual employment cases, even when the discrimination is clear and the evidence is strong, are often not large enough to make the economics work for a private plaintiff with retained counsel.

Donald Seals understood this. He had explored representation. The case had strengths. It also had the complexities inherent in contractor status disputes and the evidentiary challenges that come with fighting an institutional defendant that has control over much of the relevant documentary record.

He decided to represent himself.

This was not a decision born of desperation. It was a strategic choice made by someone who understood his own capabilities and had developed, over the preceding months, a set of tools and a methodology that gave him a genuine fighting chance.

Those tools were not what anyone would have expected.

## CHAPTER 6: BUILDING THE LEGAL MIND

### AI as Counsel

[PULL QUOTE: "The conversations happened at night, mostly. When the day's noise was gone and the only thing left was the problem and the thinking required to solve it."]

The public narrative around AI and legal work tends toward two poles. The first is the cautionary tale -- attorneys who cited hallucinated cases, systems that produced confident nonsense, the risks of relying on a technology that does not know what it does not know. The second is the transformative promise -- AI that democratizes legal access, that levels the playing field between institutional defendants and individual plaintiffs.

What happened in this case was neither narrative, exactly. It was something more specific and more human than either.

Donald Seals began using AI models as thinking partners in the fall of 2023, shortly after filing the complaint. He was not using them to generate legal arguments from nothing. He was using them the way a skilled litigator uses a junior associate: to research, to organize, to stress-test arguments, to model opposing counsel's likely responses, to flag procedural requirements he might miss, to build the scaffolding on which his own legal thinking could develop.

The first major framework was what became known as DART -- Data Analysis and Reasoning Technology. It was not a product. It was a system -- a set of structured rules and protocols that governed how AI models engaged with the discovery record. It defined how documents were tagged, how contradictions were flagged, how the evidence was organized into categories that mapped to the legal framework required to survive summary judgment.

DART had versions. It had rule groups. It had an update memo. It had a November 2025 revision that was distributed to multiple AI models simultaneously with instructions to apply the updated framework to the discovery record.

This is not a metaphor for how one person used AI to help with a case. This is a description of an actual governance system -- complete with version control, compliance rules, and cross-model coordination -- that a pro se plaintiff built and deployed to manage a federal civil rights case.

### The Sessions

[PULL QUOTE: "Every session started with the record. Every session ended with a next step. That is the discipline that turns a conversation into a case."]

The AI sessions that built this case were not casual. They were structured, methodical, and cumulative.

A typical session in the discovery phase began with a document upload -- a discovery production batch, a DHHS response to interrogatories, a new set of request for admission responses. The session would run the production through the DART framework: tagging each document by category, identifying admissions that could be locked in, flagging contradictions between what DHHS had asserted in one response and what it had admitted in another, noting deficiencies in productions that would require follow-up.

The output was not a legal argument. It was a structured record of what the evidence showed. The legal argument came from that record, shaped by Seals' own thinking and his developing understanding of the doctrines at issue.

Here is what one of those sessions looked like, drawn from the record of the process:

The November 2025 DART update session. Seals was working through the Clarity data production -- the native spreadsheet exports that DHHS had finally produced after months of dispute. The session involved multiple AI models running in parallel: ChatGPT for drafting and summarization, Gemini for document review and email analysis, Qwen for structural compliance auditing. Each model received the same DART framework update and was directed to apply it to its assigned portion of the production.

The result was a comparative hour analysis -- the document that became Exhibit D in the summary judgment package. The analysis showed, numerically, the disparity between Traub's overtime approvals and Seals' zero-overtime record. It showed the weeks, the percentages, the supervisory chain that ran through both contractors. It showed the data as data, without argument, letting the numbers make the case that words had failed to make clearly enough before.

That document was drafted in an AI session. It was verified by Seals against the underlying spreadsheet data. It was filed in federal court under penalty of perjury.

The boundary between AI assistance and human authorship was real and maintained. Every claim in every filing was verified against the record. Every legal argument was assessed by Seals himself against his understanding of the doctrine. The AI was the architecture. Seals was the engineer.

### The DART System and Its Evolution

[PULL QUOTE: "Structure precedes scale. That is not a slogan. It is the operating principle that kept this case from collapsing under its own complexity."]

DART began as a way to manage the discovery record. It evolved into something more comprehensive.

By version 7, the DART framework had rule groups covering fourteen distinct categories of litigation function: request tracking, contradiction flagging, admission locking, defensive rebuttal strategy, case progression narrative, output formatting, special case logic specific to Seals v. DHHS, quality controls, privilege log auditing, deposition outline generation, rediscovery sequencing, factual integrity verification, legal authority validation, and -- added in version 7's Rule Group 11 -- trial preparation and pretrial conference logic.

Rule Group 11 was built after the court issued Doc 72, the Order Setting Trial and Pretrial Conference, in January 2026. The moment the trial date was confirmed, the DART framework pivoted. Rule 11.1 automated the assembly of stipulated versus controverted facts from the discovery record. Rule 11.2 generated the exhibit list from the existing evidence tags. Rule 11.3 identified adverse evidence for Motion in Limine exclusion arguments. Rule 11.4 shifted all output formatting from jury-oriented narrative to the rigid numbered Findings of Fact structure required by Chief Judge Rossiter for a bench trial.

A bench trial is a trial before a judge without a jury. Chief Judge Robert F. Rossiter, Jr., a former federal prosecutor, will hear the case, evaluate the evidence, assess the credibility of witnesses, and issue written Findings of Fact and Conclusions of Law. He is not moved by emotional narrative. He is moved by structured, record-tethered legal analysis presented in the format his courtroom expects.

DART Rule 11.4 knew this before the trial began. The system adapted.

### The Conversations That Changed the Strategy

[PULL QUOTE: "The AI did not tell him what to argue. It showed him what the record actually said. What to do with that was always his decision."]

There is a particular quality to the AI conversations that shaped this case that is difficult to convey in summary. They were not the conversations of a person asking a machine for answers. They were the conversations of a sophisticated thinker using a sophisticated tool to see a problem more clearly than he could see it alone.

One of those conversations happened in the fall of 2025, in the period when DHHS had filed its motion for summary judgment. Summary judgment is the procedural moment when the defendant asks the court to rule in its favor without a trial, on the grounds that there is no genuine dispute of material fact. For a pro se plaintiff facing a state agency with institutional resources and experienced defense counsel, summary judgment is the moment when many cases end.

Seals asked the AI to model the summary judgment scenario from DHHS's perspective. Not to rebut the motion -- to understand it. To see the case the way the Nebraska Attorney General's office saw it, with all the advantages a state defendant has in this posture and all the weaknesses a pro se plaintiff carries.

The AI produced a detailed analysis of DHHS's strongest arguments: the contractor classification issue, the weakness of the comparator evidence as it stood at that moment, the procedural compliance risks, the court's likely skepticism toward a pro se plaintiff attempting a sophisticated employment discrimination case.

Then Seals asked the AI to do something else. He asked it to identify what a plaintiff would need to show to survive that summary judgment motion.

The answer was the 56(d) strategy.

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56(d) allows a party to request that the court defer ruling on a summary judgment motion when the non-moving party cannot yet present essential facts because discovery is incomplete. It requires an affidavit identifying the specific facts that are unavailable, why they are essential, and what efforts have been made to obtain them.

The specific facts that were unavailable in November 2025 were, among other things: the native Clarity export for Kim Converse -- the supervisor whose timesheet approvals were central to the Darden employer control analysis -- and the metadata and content of the documents DHHS had withheld under privilege claims.

Seals filed the Rule 56(d) motion. DHHS opposed it. The court ruled on the summary judgment package. The case survived.

On January 9, 2026, the court issued Doc 72: the Order Setting Trial and Pretrial Conference.

The trial is set for September 14, 2026.

## CHAPTER 7: THE RECORD VS. THE STORY THEY TOLD

### What DHHS Argued and What the Record Shows

[PULL QUOTE: "The institutional story has two problems. The first is that it does not match the record. The second is that the record is going to be read aloud in a federal courtroom."]

The Nebraska Department of Health and Human Services has maintained a consistent position throughout this litigation. Donald Seals was not its employee. He was a contractor employed by Contract Staffing Specialists. His assignment ended because it ended -- contract completion, performance concerns -- and DHHS had neither the authority nor the obligation that attaches to an employment relationship.

This position has a coherent legal theory behind it. It is the theory that has worked for institutional defendants in contractor classification disputes for decades. The staffing agency structure exists, in part, precisely because it creates this legal distance. The W-2 is with CSS. The payroll is with CSS. The formal employment documentation is with CSS. The legal exposure, in theory, is with CSS.

The theory meets the record.

Kim Converse interviewed and selected Donald Seals. Kim Converse assigned his daily tasks. Kim Converse approved his hours in the Clarity system -- the same system, the same approval function, the same supervisory relationship she exercised over Trent Ballentine, the white contractor who was retained. The Clarity exports show her name on the approval records. The discovery record shows her as the point of contact for the work.

Julie Christensen directed the specifics of what Seals built. Christensen's March 25, 2021 email to CSS directed the termination at 5:00 p.m. and instructed CSS not to tell him until after that time. Christensen's email did not request that CSS evaluate Seals' performance against CSS's employment standards. It directed an outcome, at a specific time.

The Nebraska Contractor Employment Waiver -- the document Seals signed acknowledging he was a CSS employee and waiving state employment benefits -- is in the record. DHHS will use it. Seals' response is ready: the Darden test is a multi-factor holistic analysis. The Eighth Circuit's own ruling in Schwieger v. Farm Bureau explicitly states that no single factor is controlling. A benefits waiver is one factor. It does not override the functional reality of who controlled the work.

The Ghost Performance problem is perhaps the most difficult for DHHS to address in a bench trial before a judge who will be evaluating credibility. There is no document in the record -- none produced in discovery, none identified in the privilege log as existing but withheld -- that was created during Donald Seals' eight-month tenure and documents a contemporaneous performance concern.

When Julie Christensen testifies that Seals was "not working out," Chief Judge Rossiter will be looking at a record in which that characterization has no written foundation created before the decision to terminate.

A judge who is evaluating credibility will have a question. If that was the reason, why was nothing written down?

The answer that the evidence suggests is not the answer DHHS will offer.

### The Voices in the Record

[PULL QUOTE: "Discovery is not an abstract process. It is the moment when an institution has to answer for what it did and what it chose not to do."]

The discovery process in this case was contentious. It stretched across multiple rounds of production, a magistrate judge discovery conference, repeated disputes about the scope of DHHS's obligations, privilege log deficiencies that persisted across three versions of the log, and a production of native Clarity data that did not include the supervisor records most relevant to the employer control analysis.

Throughout this process, Donald Seals engaged in discovery correspondence with a consistency and precision that reflects the framework he had built. His letters to opposing counsel identified specific deficiencies, cited specific rules, proposed specific remedies, and documented the pattern of incomplete production. Those letters are in the record. They show a pro se plaintiff who understood discovery obligations at a level of specificity that is unusual even among represented parties.

The Exhibit F privilege log deficiency analysis -- filed as part of the Rule 56(d) package -- identified four specific categories of non-compliance in DHHS's privilege log. The log did not separately identify email threads. The log did not identify non-attorney participants. The descriptions were too vague to assess the privilege claims. The log used date ranges rather than specific dates. All four deficiencies were documented with citations to the applicable rule and requests for specific remediation.

DHHS's privilege log was revised three times. The deficiencies identified in Exhibit F persisted across all three versions.

These are not the filings of an amateur. They are the work of someone who built a legal understanding from scratch, over years, using every available resource -- including AI models that were themselves being directed by a framework that that person designed.

## CHAPTER 8: SEPTEMBER 14, 2026

### What Is Coming

[PULL QUOTE: "A bench trial is a reckoning. Not dramatic in the way that popular culture imagines trials. Precise. Measured. Consequential."]

## APPENDIX B: GLOSSARY OF LEGAL TERMS USED IN THIS DOCUMENT

**Bench Trial:** A trial before a judge without a jury. The judge serves as both the arbiter of law and the finder of fact.

**Comparator:** An individual who was similarly situated to the plaintiff in all material respects but who received more favorable treatment. Used to establish differential treatment in discrimination cases.

**Darden Test:** The multi-factor common law agency test established in Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co. v. Darden, 503 U.S. 318 (1992), used to determine employee status under Title VII and other federal employment statutes.

**Ghost Performance Doctrine:** The evidentiary circumstance in which an employer asserts a performance-based reason for termination that has no contemporaneous documentary support created before the termination decision was made.

**McDonnell Douglas Framework:** The burden-shifting framework established in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), governing the allocation of proof in Title VII disparate treatment cases.

**Pretext:** In employment discrimination law, a stated reason for an adverse employment action that is not the true reason, masking discriminatory motivation.

**Pro Se:** Latin for "on one's own behalf." A party who represents themselves in a legal proceeding without retained counsel.

**Rule 56(d):** Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56(d), allowing a court to defer ruling on a summary judgment motion when the non-moving party shows it cannot present facts essential to opposition without additional discovery.

**Title VII:** Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e et seq., prohibiting employment discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin.
